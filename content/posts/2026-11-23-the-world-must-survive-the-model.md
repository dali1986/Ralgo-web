Genie 4 is fascinating because it points towards the kind of art I think is coming, while being built in a way that makes that art very difficult to preserve.

Google can now take an image or a description and turn it into a world that you can enter. It runs at 24 frames per second, responds to keyboard controls and generates sound as it goes. You can change the weather with a prompt while you are inside it. You can introduce a character or ask for an event and watch the world respond.

This is a remarkable thing. It is also a cloud service that currently lasts for around a minute, costs roughly $6 for that minute and may forget what parts of its own world looked like while you are still there.

That tension feels more important to me than the demonstration itself.

## The kind of art I think is coming

I have increasingly come to believe that much of the most interesting future art will be made from systems rather than finished objects. The work will continue when nobody is watching. It may contain characters who form relationships, remember what has happened and make decisions that alter the world around them.

Imagine a world inhabited by Creatures from the Overgrowth. They build settlements and develop their own customs. Some become explorers. Others hoard resources or try to persuade neighbouring groups to cooperate. Perhaps a danger appears which none of them can deal with alone. Visitors can enter the world and affect it, although they never completely control what happens there.

That world could exist for years. A collector might own one creature and discover that it has developed a history they did not design. An exhibition could become a temporary way into the same world. The work would be the society and everything it remembers.

[Genie 4](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/) gives us a convincing glimpse of how such a place might look. Starting from a single Overgrowth image, it could generate a habitat around the creature and let somebody move through it immediately. The generated sound may be as important as the image. A place becomes much more believable when its weather can be heard beyond the frame.

There is a temptation to see this and conclude that the persistent-world problem has almost been solved. It has not.

## A world that forgets itself

Google's [own model card](https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/genie-4/) says that visual details and object permanence can drift, particularly as the session continues or the viewer moves around. Complex interaction between several independent agents remains difficult. The current model supports about one minute of continuous interaction.

A minute is enough to create the impression of a world. Persistence asks for something much harder.

If a creature walks behind a tree and returns with a different face, it has lost part of its identity. If a building changes when nobody is looking, the world cannot develop a dependable geography. Once characters begin making decisions, the problem becomes deeper. The system has to remember who promised what and whether that promise was kept. A battle needs to change the balance of power next week.

The world needs a truth that exists independently of whatever image is being generated at that moment.

This is where Genie conflicts with another idea I think matters greatly: local AI.

A serious persistent artwork should be able to carry its intelligence with it. The model, world state and rules should remain under the artist's control. A collector or museum should not discover that the work has changed because a technology company updated an API. Nor should the artwork stop functioning because the service was withdrawn or became too expensive to run.

Genie currently gives the artist no such certainty. It runs on Google's infrastructure. Its behaviour will change as the model changes. The endpoint may eventually disappear. Even if the artwork survives conceptually, the particular intelligence that animated it may be impossible to reconstruct.

## Keeping the world outside the model

Perhaps the solution, at least for now, is to separate the world from the way it appears.

The creatures and their memories could live in an authored system running locally. That system would know every relationship and the current state of each settlement. It would decide what consequences persist. Its history could be backed up, inspected and eventually transferred with the artwork.

Genie could then act as a temporary window into that world. When somebody enters, it receives the current state and turns one part of it into a visible encounter. The viewer might meet a creature who remembers a decision made by somebody several days earlier. When the minute ends, anything consequential is written back into the local world.

The visible environment may drift, but the history beneath it remains intact. If Genie disappears, another renderer could eventually inhabit the same world. The appearance would change. The society and its accumulated past would survive.

I find that architecture quite interesting in its own right. The world has a persistent reality, although every visit to it is unstable. This may feel closer to memory than a perfectly fixed 3D environment would. Places are not experienced in exactly the same way twice, even when we believe they remain the same place.

There is still a danger here. Once Genie becomes central to how the work feels, losing it may amount to losing a significant part of the artwork. Calling it a replaceable renderer does not make the dependency disappear. The local system therefore needs to remain meaningful by itself. It should be possible to enter through a simpler renderer and still encounter the same underlying life.

## The cost of keeping a window open

The [current API price](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing#genie-4) is approximately $0.10 per second, including generated audio. A full minute costs about $6.05. One hundred visits would cost roughly $605 before paying for any of the other systems around the work.

That makes an always-running online world unrealistic. It may still support a work built around brief encounters. A museum could open the window a limited number of times each day. A collector might enter for one minute and then wait while the world continues locally without them.

The restriction could even produce a useful artistic form. We are used to digital work being permanently available and infinitely repeatable. A world that opens briefly and then closes might feel more consequential, particularly if it continues to change while we are away.

I would still treat Genie as a research instrument today. I would take one strong Overgrowth creature, give it a small habitat and define a few facts that must never change. Then I would run the same encounter repeatedly and look for the moment when the creature stops feeling like itself.

The most useful result may be discovering what has to remain outside the world model.

Genie 4 gives us an extraordinary preview of generated worlds. It also demonstrates why the lasting artwork needs a foundation the artist can own. The world must keep its memories when the model changes, and it must survive when the cloud closes.
